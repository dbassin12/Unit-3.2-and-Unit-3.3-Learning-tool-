Part 1: The Multiplier Effect

How a single dollar ripples through the entire economy

💰 Disposable Income

Disposable Income is your after-tax income — the money you actually get to keep. You can only do two things with it:

1. Spend it (Consumption)    2. Save it (Savings)

Example: You earn $500 at your summer job. Taxes take $75. Your disposable income is $425. You spend $350 on sneakers, food, and gas, and save $75.

MPC

The Marginal Propensity to Consume is the fraction of every extra dollar you spend.

MPC = ΔC / ΔDI

If MPC = 0.90, you spend 90¢ of every extra dollar.

MPS

The Marginal Propensity to Save is the fraction of every extra dollar you save.

MPS = ΔS / ΔDI

If MPS = 0.10, you save 10¢ of every extra dollar.

The Golden Rule

MPC + MPS = 1

You either spend it or save it. There's no third option!

🌊 The Ripple Effect

One person's spending becomes another person's income. Your spending at Chipotle pays the worker's wage. That worker spends their paycheck at Target. The Target employee spends at the gas station... and so on.

Spending Multiplier

1 / MPS   or   1 / (1 − MPC)

If MPC = 0.80: 1 / 0.20 = 5

The LESS people save, the BIGGER the multiplier.

Tax Multiplier

−MPC / MPS   or   −MPC / (1 − MPC)

If MPC = 0.80: −0.80 / 0.20 = −4

Tax cuts are positive (+). Tax increases are negative (−).

Why Is the Tax Multiplier Smaller?

When the government spends $100, all $100 enters the economy immediately. But when the government cuts taxes by $100, people save part of it first, so less enters the spending stream.

The spending multiplier is always exactly 1 MORE than the tax multiplier.

Balanced Budget Multiplier

When ΔG = ΔT (government increases spending AND taxes by the same amount):

Balanced Budget Multiplier = 1

Always. Regardless of MPC. A $500B increase in G with a $500B tax increase = $500B ΔAD.

Part 2: Short-Run Aggregate Supply

Understanding the supply side of the economy

🏭 Aggregate Supply

Aggregate Supply = the total level of Real GDP that all firms will produce at each price level.

Long-Run (LRAS)

FLEXIBLE PRICES

In the long run, ALL input prices (wages, rent, materials) fully adjust to price level changes. Real profits don't change, so output stays at full employment (Yf).

LRAS is VERTICAL — like the PPC.

Short-Run (SRAS)

STICKY PRICES

In the short run, input prices are sticky — wages and contracts take time to adjust. When PL rises, costs stay the same, so profits rise and firms produce more.

SRAS is UPWARD SLOPING.

🍕 The Pizza Shop Analogy

Imagine you own a pizza shop. Your costs are sticky: rent is locked in ($2K/month), workers are at $15/hr (contract), and flour was ordered at last month's price.

Now the price level rises and you can charge $15/pizza instead of $12. Your costs haven't changed, but your revenue went up! So you hire more workers and make more pizzas.

This is exactly why SRAS slopes upward: sticky costs + rising prices = higher profits = more output.

What Shifts SRAS?

Everything comes down to: Per-Unit Production Cost = Total Input Cost / Total Output

1. Input Prices — Wages (75% of costs!), raw materials, energy, foreign resources
2. Productivity — Total output / Total inputs (technology, training, efficiency)
3. Legal-Institutional Environment — Business taxes, subsidies, government regulation

SRAS Shifts RIGHT →

Lower per-unit costs:

  • Wages decrease
  • Raw material prices fall
  • Productivity increases (AI, robots, training)
  • Business tax cuts / subsidies
  • Deregulation
  • Strong dollar (cheaper imports)

← SRAS Shifts LEFT

Higher per-unit costs:

  • Wages increase
  • Raw material prices rise
  • Productivity decreases
  • Business tax increases
  • More regulation (compliance costs)
  • Weak dollar (pricier imports)

7 Key Takeaways

  1. MPC + MPS = 1. You either spend or save every dollar.
  2. Spending Multiplier = 1/MPS. Tax Multiplier = −MPC/MPS.
  3. Spending mult. > Tax mult. (always by exactly 1).
  4. Balanced Budget Multiplier = 1. Always.
  5. SRAS slopes up because input costs are sticky in the short run.
  6. SRAS shifts when per-unit production costs change.
  7. 3 SRAS shifters: Input prices, Productivity, Legal environment.
