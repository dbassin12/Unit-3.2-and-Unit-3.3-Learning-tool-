Unit 3.2

The Multiplier Effect

How a single dollar ripples through the entire economy, creating way more than its face value in total spending.

💰

Disposable Income

Your disposable income is your after-tax paycheck. The money you actually get to use. You do exactly two things with it:

🛍️
Spend It
Consumption (C)
🏦
Save It
Savings (S)
📈

MPC

Marginal Propensity to Consume: the fraction of every extra dollar you spend.

MPC = ΔC / ΔDI
Your grandma gives you $100. You spend $80 on stuff and save $20. Your MPC = 0.80.
💎

MPS

Marginal Propensity to Save: the fraction of every extra dollar you save.

MPS = ΔS / ΔDI
Same example: you saved $20 of the $100. Your MPS = 0.20.
MPC + MPS = 1

You either spend it or save it. There is no third option.

MPC = 1 − MPS   |   MPS = 1 − MPC
🔄

The Spending Multiplier

When someone spends money, it becomes someone else's income. That person spends part of it, which becomes another person's income... This chain reaction means an initial change in spending causes a LARGER change in Aggregate Demand.

Multiplier = 1 / MPS

or equivalently: 1 / (1 − MPC)

ΔAD = Δ Spending × Multiplier

applies to changes in C, I, G, or Xn

Key Insight: The LESS people save (smaller MPS), the BIGGER the multiplier. If everyone spent every dime (MPC=1), the multiplier would be infinite!
📋

The Tax Multiplier

Taxes pull money OUT of the circular flow. The tax multiplier is always smaller than the spending multiplier (by exactly 1) because when people get a tax cut, they save part of it first.

Tax Increase (−)
Tax Mult = −MPC / MPS
Tax Cut (+)
Tax Mult = +MPC / MPS
⚖️

Balanced Budget Multiplier

When government spending increases by the SAME amount as a tax increase (ΔG = ΔT), the multipliers partially cancel out:

Balanced Budget Multiplier = 1

The change in AD always equals the change in government spending. Always. Every time. Regardless of MPC.

Why? 1/MPS + (−MPC/MPS) = (1 − MPC)/MPS = MPS/MPS = 1

Part 2: Short-Run Aggregate Supply

Understanding the supply side of the macroeconomy

Unit 3.3

Aggregate Supply

The total Real GDP that firms produce at each price level. It behaves very differently in the short run vs. the long run.

📏

LRAS (Long-Run)

Input prices are fully flexible. Wages, contracts, and materials all adjust.

If prices rise, costs rise equally. Real profits stay the same, so output doesn't change.

LRAS is VERTICAL
at full employment (Yf)
Think of it like the PPC
📐

SRAS (Short-Run)

Input prices are sticky. Wages and contracts take time to renegotiate.

If prices rise but costs stay fixed, firms earn more profit and produce more.

SRAS slopes UPWARD
higher PL → more output
Sticky costs are the key
🍕

The Pizza Shop Analogy

You own a pizza shop. Your rent ($2,000/mo) is locked in a lease. Workers earn $15/hr by contract. Flour was ordered at last month's price.

Now imagine the price level rises and you can charge $15/pizza instead of $12. Your costs haven't changed yet, but revenue is up! So you hire more workers and make more pizza.

This is EXACTLY why SRAS slopes upward: sticky costs + rising prices = higher profits = more output.

↔️

What Shifts SRAS?

Everything comes back to one concept: per-unit production cost (total input cost ÷ total output).

1. Input Prices

Wages (75% of costs!), raw materials, energy, and foreign resource prices. Strong dollar = cheaper imports = SRAS right.

2. Productivity

More output per input = lower cost per unit. New tech (AI, robots), better training → SRAS shifts right.

3. Legal Environment

Business taxes ↑ costs (SRAS left). Subsidies ↓ costs (SRAS right). Regulation ↑ compliance costs (SRAS left).
